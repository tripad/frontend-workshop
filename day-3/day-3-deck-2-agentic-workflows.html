Agentic Workflows

The new design surface.

We've spent two days designing screens with AI's help. Now the harder question — what happens when AI isn't just helping you build a screen, but taking actions on its own?

This deck is short on technical detail and long on lens. The point isn't to make you build agents. It's to show you why "designer of screens" is becoming "designer of workflows" — and why that's the most consequential shift in product design since mobile.

If we had another day, we'd build a small agentic workflow together. We don't, so we'll talk about it.

The shift

AI suggests → AI does.

A small grammar change. A massive product shift.

Old AIsuggests

  • Drafts your email.
  • Suggests three colors.
  • Autocompletes your code.

You read. You decide. You act.

New AIdoes

  • Sends the email.
  • Books the flight.
  • Refactors the codebase. Files the ticket.

You set the goal. It acts. You verify.

The difference isn't model size. It's agency — the system was given tools, given permission, and given a reason to take action without checking back at every step.

tool → worker

When AI suggests, it's a tool. When AI does, it's a worker. Designing for a tool and designing for a worker are different problems.

The unit of design changes

From screens to workflows.

When agents act, the unit of design changes.

screens

→

workflows

For twenty years, design was about screens. Wireframes. Mockups. Prototypes. Each frame was a place a user looked.

When the system can act across multiple steps and tools, the unit of design isn't a screen anymore. It's a workflow — a sequence of decisions, actions, and handoffs, with humans showing up only at certain moments.

The deliverable changes. The vocabulary changes. The job changes.

Screens didn't go away. But they're now one artifact among several — sitting alongside workflow diagrams, prompt specs, tool schemas, and confirmation states. Designers who only design screens will keep designing screens. Designers who can also design workflows will be working on the things screens were never enough for.

The artifact

What does a workflow look like?

Boxes and arrows. With teeth.

A workflow is a sequence of steps connected by triggers, actions, and decisions.

Tools like n8n, Zapier, and Make let you draw these directly. Tools like Inngest and LangGraph let you code them. Tools like Claude with MCP let you describe them in language. The artifact looks deceptively simple. The hard work is in the branches, the failure paths, and the moments where a human steps in.

A reference card

Two new audiences.

Designers serve humans. Now they also serve agents.

Agents as users.

Your product is consumed by an agent. Stripe's API documentation is now read by AI tools as much as by engineers. Search results are summarized by AI before users see them. Your SaaS product gets queried by a Claude that's filing a report for someone else.

Designing for agents means clean semantics, predictable structure, machine-readable affordances, well-described tools. The web's messiness is becoming a liability — agents need order.

Humans assisted by agents.

Your user delegates work to an agent. Antigravity. Granola. Cursor. Devin. All of these put a human and an agent in collaboration on a single task.

Designing for this means deciding: what does the human see while the agent works? When does it pause for confirmation? What's the undo affordance? When does it say "I'm not sure — you handle this"? These are interaction-design questions, not engineering ones.

A reference card

The new design vocabulary.

Words your screens didn't need.

When you design workflows, you'll need words you didn't need before. A starter set:

  • →Triggerwhat kicks the workflow off
  • →Stepa discrete action the agent takes
  • →Brancha decision point: the agent picks, or it asks
  • →Toolsomething the agent is allowed to call
  • →Confirmationa pause for human approval
  • →Handoffwork passes from agent to human, or agent to agent
  • →Exception pathwhat happens when something goes wrong
  • →Outcomewhat "done" actually looks like

Most of these used to be engineering concerns. They aren't anymore. If you can't reason about confirmation moments and exception paths, you can't design the product.

The career angle

Workflows are a designable thing.

Almost no one is doing it well yet.

The product teams that ship the best agentic experiences over the next five years will be the ones with designers who can think in workflows, not just screens. Right now, that skill barely exists.

You don't need to learn to build agents. You need to learn to:

  • →Read a workflow diagram and tell whether it's good or bad
  • →Spot the missing confirmation moment
  • →Notice when the human side of the experience is an afterthought
  • →Argue for the cut that takes a 12-step workflow down to 5

You're walking into it.

Takeaway

Screens are still here.

They're just not the whole job anymore.

Designers built careers designing screens. That work didn't disappear.

But the next generation of products is being built around what happens between screens — the workflows, the agents, the moments where the system acts without you watching.

Learning to design those moments is the skill that separates the designers who keep up from the designers who get caught up.
